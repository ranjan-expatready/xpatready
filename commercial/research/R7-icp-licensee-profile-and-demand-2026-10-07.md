# R7 — Ideal-customer profile (ICP): licensee profile and demand drivers

**Date:** 7 Oct 2026. **Writer:** research agent (Claude). **Status:** research input only — nothing here is a decision. Every figure was checked on 7 Oct 2026 against the source listed in the Sources table (S-numbers). Aggregates only: no register or directory was scraped or downloaded, no individual was profiled, nobody was contacted, no form was submitted.

**In plain words:**
- *Licensee* = a person licensed by the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants (CICC, "the College"). *RCIC* = Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultant. *RCIC-IRB* = an RCIC also licensed to appear before the Immigration and Refugee Board (the refugee and appeals tribunal). *RISIA* = Regulated International Student Immigration Advisor (advises on student immigration matters only).
- *In good standing* = licensed and allowed to practise on that date.
- *Levels Plan* = the federal government's yearly target for how many permanent and temporary residents Canada admits.
- *Derived* = my own arithmetic from published figures; the formula is shown.
- The College's fiscal year runs 1 July to 30 June, so "2025" below means the count on 30 June 2025.

---

## 0. Answer to the founder's question, and the five most important findings

**How many RCICs are there?** **11,994 RCICs in good standing on 30 June 2025** (S1), plus **507 RISIAs** (S1) — about **12,500 licensees in all** (derived: 11,994 + 507 = 12,501). The College's internal registration report counts 12,096 RCICs and 12,608 licensees in total on the same date, which probably includes licensees not in good standing; the source does not explain the gap (S4). **Neither 25,000 nor 10,000 is right.** 25,000 is about double the real figure; 10,000 is about 17% too low. And the number of *practices* (businesses that would buy software) is smaller than the number of licensees — by how much is **not published**.

1. **The profession stopped growing in 2024 and shrank slightly in 2025.** Licensees nearly doubled from 5,923 (2019) to 11,698 (2022) (S6), peaked at 12,264 RCICs in 2024, then fell to 11,994 in 2025 (−2.2%, derived) (S1, S2). New RCIC licences fell from 1,504 (FY2023) to 626 (FY2024) to 470 (FY2025) — down 69% (derived) — while 753 RCICs left in FY2025 (6% attrition) (S1–S3). The College's own board papers describe the trend as flat (S4, S5).
2. **Customers are concentrated in three provinces.** Ontario 5,391 (44.9%), British Columbia 2,963 (24.7%), Alberta 1,588 (13.2%) — 83% of all RCICs (derived). Quebec has 1,097 (9.1%) and is growing; only 261 RCICs (2.2%) live outside Canada (S1).
3. **The base is young in the profession.** 54% of RCICs have practised 0–4 years; only 18% have 10+ years (S1). 53% are women; 53% are aged 40–59 (S1). These are the only demographics the College publishes.
4. **Refugee and tribunal work is the fastest-growing licence class.** RCIC-IRB licences rose from 704 (2023) to 1,358 (2024) to 2,076 (2025) — now 17% of RCICs (derived) (S1–S3). This matches the demand side: 171,840 asylum claims in 2024 (S18) and a one-time move of about 115,000 protected persons to permanent residence (S16).
5. **Demand is shifting from new arrivals to people already in Canada.** Permanent-resident targets are steady at 380,000 a year for 2026–2028 (S16), but new temporary arrivals are being cut hard: new student arrivals were 80% lower in January–July 2026 than in the same months of 2024 (S19). People already in Canada made up 57% of new permanent residents in January–July 2026 (S19). Expect more extension and transition work and fewer new study-permit files. This is an observation, not a forecast.

**What we do not know:** the number of firms and their sizes, owner versus employee mix, caseloads, fees, software use, practice-area mix and languages of service. **None of these is published** by the College, IRCC or a credible survey that could be found (section 4 and section 6).

**Caveat — do not use this as a profile of RCICs.** In June 2021 the regulator (then the ICCRC, the College's predecessor) counted 8,288 licensees (S6). ### 3.3 Share of applications filed with a paid representative

**Not published by IRCC as an official statistic.** The College's 2025 annual report says, citing IRCC data, "only a small percentage of newcomers engage a lawyer or consultant" (S1, p.4), but gives no number. A Vancouver immigration lawyer's blog gives 2018 counts by representative type without a source, and its figures do not add up (S26) — **do not use**. A 2013 government evaluation said the data were too limited to measure use of consultants (S27). **Lawful route:** an access-to-information request to IRCC for *aggregate* counts of applications by representative type (paid consultant, lawyer, unpaid, none) by program and year.

### 3.4 Unauthorised representatives and new rules

- The College shut down 5,390 websites and social-media pages run by unauthorised practitioners in FY2025, and 7,636 over two years (S1).
- IRCC says it investigated an average of over 9,000 suspected immigration-fraud cases a month in 2024 (S22).
- New College regulations took effect on **15 July 2026**: a compensation fund for clients, stronger discipline powers, and more information on the public register (including business names) from **April 2027** (S8, S9). The government notes that licensees are, in many cases, small businesses, and that the College may raise annual fees to pay for the changes (S8).
- IRCC has proposed administrative penalties for paid representatives who break the rules, authorised or not: $5,000 per case of unauthorised practice, $15,000 per case of encouraging misrepresentation, up to $1.5 million in total (S23, S25). Final rules are targeted for Canada Gazette Part II in **fall 2026** (S24) — **not yet in force; confirm with counsel**.

### 3.5 What this implies for practice volumes (observations, not forecasts)

1. **Total demand is lower and changing in kind, not growing.** Permanent-resident targets fell from 485,000 to 380,000 a year. New temporary arrivals are down by half or more. Fewer licensees (−2.2%) are sharing a smaller pool of new-arrival files.
2. **The work is moving toward people already in Canada:** permit extensions, temporary-to-permanent transitions (57% of new permanent residents), provincial nominations (back up to 91,500), protected persons, and refused or complex files (study refusals at 59%).
3. **Tribunal and refugee work is rising on both sides:** RCIC-IRB licences tripled in two years, alongside high asylum volumes and the one-time protected-persons measure.
4. **The compliance load is rising** (compensation fund, discipline powers, public-register detail, proposed IRCC penalties). This may raise practice costs and increase the value of record-keeping and audit-ready workflows. Treat that as a hypothesis to test in interviews.
