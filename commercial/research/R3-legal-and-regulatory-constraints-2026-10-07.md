# R3 — Legal and regulatory constraints on the commercial plan (Canada)

**Date:** 7 Oct 2026. **Writer:** research agent (Claude). **Status:** research input only. **This is not legal or tax advice.** It collects rules from primary sources so counsel (and, for tax, an accountant) can confirm them. Every row is marked **confirm with counsel**. Nothing here decides anything.
**Scope:** seven topics that touch the commercial plan. These are: (1) the RCIC Code, (2) lawyers' codes, (3) anti-spam law (CASL), (4) the Competition Act, (5) privacy, (6) tax on referral rewards, (7) securities rules for a future fundraise.
**Checked:** every source was read on **7 Oct 2026**. The version or date shown on each source is in the Sources list (S1–S33). Table cells cite the source ID and section.

**In plain words**
- *RCIC* = Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultant. RCICs are licensed by the *CICC* (College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants). The CICC *Code* is a federal regulation, and breaching it is professional misconduct (CICC Act s. 44).
- *Inducement* = any benefit given for a referral. The CICC guide includes commissions, bonuses and discounts.
- *CASL* = Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation. A *CEM* (commercial electronic message) is an email, text or direct message that promotes a product. The *CRTC* enforces CASL.
- *Competition Bureau* = the federal agency that enforces the rules on false or misleading advertising.
- *Material connection* = a link between a person who recommends something and the seller, such as a commission or a credit. Readers would weigh the recommendation differently if they knew about it.
- *PIPEDA* = the federal private-sector privacy law. *Law 25* = the 2021 Quebec law that amended Quebec's private-sector privacy act. *CAI* = Quebec's privacy regulator.
- *T4A* = the CRA slip used to report some payments to non-employees. *Small supplier* = a business under the $30,000 GST/HST registration threshold.
- *NI 31-103* = the national rule on who must register to deal in securities. *Business trigger* = the test for being "in the business" of trading. *EMD* = exempt market dealer, a registered dealer for private placements. *NI 45-106* = the national rule on *prospectus exemptions*, which let a company raise money without a full public prospectus. A *finder* is an unregistered person paid to introduce investors.

**Answer to task 1, stated carefully.** (a) No Code section was found that expressly covers an RCIC receiving a commission or subscription credit for recommending software to **other licensees**. Both situations appear to fall outside the literal text of ss. 13, 44 and 45. They remain within the general duties in s. 4 (honour, conduct unbecoming), s. 9 (obeying the law), s. 43 (statements about licensees) and s. 46 (College marks). They also fall within ss. 13, 15 and 16 if clients are involved. **Confirm with counsel.**

## 3. CASL (anti-spam) and CRTC guidance

| Rule | Source / section | What it appears to mean for Expat Ready | Questions for counsel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What a CEM is.** A message that, judged by its content, links or contact details, has "as its purpose, or one of its purposes, to encourage participation in a commercial activity". ## 4. Competition Act and Competition Bureau guidance

| Rule | Source / section | What it appears to mean for Expat Ready | Questions for counsel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **False or misleading representations.** It is reviewable conduct to make a representation to the public "that is false or misleading in a material respect". It becomes a criminal offence if done "knowingly or recklessly". The "general impression" counts as well as the "literal meaning". Proof that anyone was deceived is not needed. | S13 s. 74.01(1)(a), (3.2), (4); s. 52(1), (4); S18 | This applies to every website, deck and outreach claim. **The overall impression matters**, not only the fine print. | Do representations made only to businesses count as made "to the public"? **Confirm with counsel.** |
| **False claims in electronic messages.** It is reviewable to send a false or misleading representation in an electronic message. This includes the sender and subject line. | S13 s. 74.011(1)–(2) | Subject lines such as "your IRCC compliance update" for a sales email appear risky. | **Confirm with counsel.** |
| **Performance claims need prior testing.** It is reviewable to make a claim about "the performance, efficacy or length of life of a product" that is "not based on an adequate and proper test… the proof of which lies on the person making the representation". The Bureau says the test "must have been done before the claim is made". | S13 s. 74.01(1)(b); S14 | **"99.9% uptime", time-saving and security-strength claims appear to be performance claims.** They would need evidence before publication, such as monitoring data, tests or audit reports. | What evidence is "adequate and proper" for uptime and security claims about software? **Confirm with counsel.** |
| **Guarantees.** It is reviewable to present something as "a warranty or guarantee of a product" if it "is materially misleading or if there is no reasonable prospect that it will be carried out". | S13 s. 74.01(1)(c) | **"99.9% SLA"** presented as a guarantee appears to need an actual, enforceable SLA. That means written terms, a way to measure it, and stated remedies such as service credits. | **Confirm with counsel.** |
| **Ordinary price and savings.** A claim about the price at which a product "or like products" are ordinarily supplied must pass a **volume test** (a substantial volume sold) or a **time test** (offered in good faith for a substantial period). The Bureau's guidelines treat a substantial period as more than 50% of the time over six months. The Bureau says the Act prohibits "unsubstantiated savings claims" and recommends a compliance programme. | S13 s. 74.01(2)–(3); S15 §4.1, 4.2.2.2 (guidelines of 16 Oct 2009); S17 | **"7-tool baseline $482/seat/mo" and "~59% savings"** compare Expat Ready with a sum of other vendors' prices. They appear to need dated proof of each tool's list price for the same plan level, the seat basis and the currency. The general impression should match a typical practice. If the page suggests every practice pays all seven tools, that impression must be true. | Is a sum of competitors' prices a "like products" ordinary-price claim, or a general false-or-misleading matter? **Confirm with counsel.** |
| **Drip pricing.** A price "that is not attainable due to fixed obligatory charges or fees" is a false or misleading representation. | S13 s. 74.01(1.1); s. 52(1.3) | The advertised per-seat price must include any mandatory set-up, onboarding or minimum-seat charges. | **Confirm with counsel.** |
| **Testimonials and tests.** Publishing a testimonial or test claim is reviewable unless it was previously published by the person who gave it, or that person "approved and permission… was given in writing". | S13 s. 74.02 | Get written approval from each RCIC or lawyer who is quoted. Keep the wording identical to what they approved. | **Confirm with counsel.** |
| **Material connections (endorsements, influencers, affiliates).** The Bureau says to "disclose all material connections". Examples are "payment in money or commissions" and "discounts". Disclosures should be "prominent". Even "linking to an affiliate webpage is unlikely to be enough". The Digest adds that "advertisers may be liable for representations made through influencers". | S16 (modified 2022-01-19); S17 (Digest Vol. 4, page modified 2026-09-09) | **RCIC referrers who post or message about Expat Ready appear to need to disclose their commission or credit** in each post and message. Expat Ready appears to need to require this, check for it, and stop referrers from making performance claims on its behalf. | Is a one-to-one peer email "to the public" for this purpose? **Confirm with counsel.** |
| **Penalties.** For a corporation, the court may order the greater of "$10,000,000" ("$15,000,000" for later orders) and three times the benefit, or "3% of the corporation's annual worldwide gross revenues". Restitution and orders lasting 10 years are also possible. Criminal s. 52 carries fines or up to 14 years' imprisonment. **Any person may seek leave** to apply to the Competition Tribunal under s. 74.1. | S13 s. 74.1(1)–(2); s. 52(5); s. 103.1(1) | Competitors could bring deceptive-marketing applications themselves, with the Tribunal's permission. | When did private access for s. 74.1 come into force? **Confirm with counsel.** |

## 5. Privacy: B2B prospect and lead data

| Rule | Source / section | What it appears to mean for Expat Ready | Questions for counsel |

## Design implications for the referral programme, outreach, website claims and fundraising

All of these are conditional and need counsel's confirmation.

**Referral programme**
1. **If** counsel confirms that CICC s. 13 is limited to client referrals, the programme could reward **peer-firm referrals only**. It would then never reward a referrer for recommending Expat Ready to their own immigration clients, and never pass client leads (ss. 13, 15, 16).
2. **If** the CICC would see credits as inducements in any client setting, credits do not reduce the professional-conduct risk compared with cash. The choice between credit and cash should then be driven by tax and administration (accountant).
3. **If** referrers post or message about Expat Ready, the terms could require a plain disclosure of the material connection in every post and message, such as "I receive a credit from Expat Ready". The terms could also ban performance claims and allow monitoring and removal from the programme (Bureau S16, S17).
4. **If** Expat Ready contacts referred peers itself, the third-party referral exemption allows **one** first email. It must name the referrer, say it was sent because of the referral, and include identification and unsubscribe details. Later messages need consent. The referrer would attest to their relationship with the peer (S7 s. 4).
5. **If** "invite a colleague" emails are sent from the product on a referrer's behalf, Expat Ready may be the person who "causes or permits" them. Such features should be reviewed under CASL s. 9 and the personal-relationship definition before they are built.
6. **If** referrers are RCICs, any CASL or Competition Act breach they commit may also engage their own Code duties (s. 9, s. 4(2)). Short compliance training for referrers would help both sides.

**Outreach**
7. **If** relying on the published-address route, store evidence for each contact: URL, screenshot, date, absence of a no-solicitation note, and why the message is relevant to their role. Purchased or scraped lists, and directory-only addresses, should be cleared with counsel first.
8. **If** LinkedIn messages are CEMs, as the CRTC FAQ extract suggests, the same consent, identification and unsubscribe rules apply. Templates should carry them.
9. **If** events produce business cards or sign-ups, record the event and date, which supports s. 10(9)(c). Use a separate consent tick box where possible.
10. **If** a written CASL compliance programme exists, it supports the due-diligence defence (s. 33). It should cover policy, training, consent records, a 10-business-day unsubscribe process and checks on referrers.

**Website claims**
11. **If** "$482/seat/mo" and "~59%" stay, keep a dated evidence file for each. It should name the seven tools, the plan levels, the list prices, the seat basis, the date and currency, and the assumption that a practice uses all seven. State the basis next to the claim, since the general impression is what counts. Re-check prices on a set schedule.
12. **If** "99.9% SLA" stays, publish actual SLA terms (measurement, exclusions, credits). Do not imply historical uptime without monitoring data (s. 74.01(1)(b), (c)).
13. **If** security claims name standards or audits, such as encryption, SOC 2 or Canadian hosting, each should be true on the day it is published and backed by documents. "Coming soon" features should be labelled as such.
14. **If** RCIC or lawyer testimonials are used, get written approval of the exact wording (s. 74.02). Avoid any wording that implies approval by the CICC, IRCC or a law society (CICC s. 44(2)(c) by analogy; Competition Act s. 74.01).
15. **If** there are mandatory set-up or minimum-seat charges, the advertised per-seat price needs to include them (drip pricing).

**Founder (RCIC) and privacy**
16. **If** the founder keeps practising, keep the software business separate from his consulting practice. Check use of the CICC logo or RCIC insignia (s. 46). Get counsel's view before selling to anyone who is also his immigration client (s. 16).
17. **If** Quebec prospects are targeted, name and publish a privacy officer. Do a privacy impact assessment and sign written agreements before storing their data outside Quebec. Write separate, purpose-specific consent wording. Separately, Quebec's French-language rules for commercial material were **not researched**.

**Fundraising**
18. **If** advisers or introducers are paid on success, use only registered dealers (for example an EMD), checked on the National Registration Search, unless counsel confirms an exemption. Use named prospectus exemptions with the right forms and file reports within 10 days. Under the private issuer exemption, do not pay commissions to insiders.
